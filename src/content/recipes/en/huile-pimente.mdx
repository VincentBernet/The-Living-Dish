steps:
  - Thinly slice the garlic, fry it for 4 to 5 min in the neutral oil until lightly browned, then set it aside.
  - Dry-toast the Sichuan pepper for 30 to 40 s in a very hot pan (until it starts to release its aroma), then set it aside. Do the same with the sesame seeds (1 to 2 min), then the peanuts (2 to 3 min) (for a light color and a nice toasted aroma; skip this step if they are already toasted).
  - Toast the remaining spices that will infuse the oil for 30 to 40 s (cardamom, cinnamon, black pepper, star anise, coriander seeds), then roughly chop the ginger (without peeling it) and the scallions.
  - Infuse the spices from step 3 in the oil over low to medium heat for 8 min, with the ginger and scallions.
  - Meanwhile grind the peanuts, Sichuan pepper, sesame seeds, and previously fried garlic.
  - Fill the jar with the sugar, salt, ground mixture, soy sauce, black rice vinegar, gochugaru, and Cayenne pepper.
  - Pour the hot oil into the jar while straining out the spices.

## A recipe inspired by the Sichuan region

In Sichuan cuisine, chili oil is a central preparation used in countless dishes.
They do not use just one, but rather a wide range of different oils: Hóngyóu (红油), Málà hóngyóu (麻辣红油), Lǎoyóu (老油), Pàojiāo yóu (泡椒油), Xiānjiāo yóu (鲜椒油).
This is not a step-by-step recipe for one of these oils, but rather a recipe inspired by these different preparations and their particular techniques, an in-between blend of Sichuan chili oil and chili crisp oil.

When choosing chilies, we are looking for three functions: heat, aroma and flavor, and color. So potentially three different chilies for their respective qualities.
Here, Gochugaru (Korean chili flakes) provides both color and aroma; it is only mildly spicy, which is why we use so much of it.
Cayenne, on the other hand, brings significant heat, so use a slightly lighter hand and adjust the quantity according to your guests' tolerance.

The choice of oil is also important. There are two possibilities. Either use a neutral oil that is easy to find in supermarkets (grapeseed, rapeseed, or sunflower oil), or a more aromatic and traditional oil, which is also more difficult to find. Namely caiziyou (菜籽油), a toasted rapeseed oil.
This one is very common in Sichuan cuisine, with a powerful, toasted, slightly vegetal flavor and a mustard aroma when heated (unlike neutral oils, which are more odorless).

When serving the chili oil in your cooking or at the table, you can either scoop out only the oil from the top of the jar or take some of the solids, which are absolutely delicious and very fragrant.
