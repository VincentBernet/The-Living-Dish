steps:
  - Thinly slice the garlic, fry it for 4 to 5 min in the neutral oil (around 150 °C) until the slices are lightly browned, then set the garlic aside.
  - Dry-toast the spices that will be used to infuse the oil (cardamom, cinnamon, black pepper, star anise, coriander seeds) for 30 to 40 s, then roughly slice the ginger (without peeling it) and the scallions.
  - Add these spices from the previous step, the ginger, and the scallions to the oil over low to medium heat (110–120 °C) for 30 to 60 min. A very gentle simmer should occur; if it simmers more strongly, lower the heat or, if you are already at the lowest setting, remove it from the heat for a few moments.
  - Meanwhile, dry-toast the Sichuan pepper for 30 to 40 s, stirring constantly in your hot pan (over medium heat, until its aroma starts to develop), then set it aside. Do the same with the sesame seeds (1 to 2 min), then the peanuts (2 to 3 min, for a light color and a nice toasted aroma; if they are already toasted commercially, skip this step). Then roughly crush the peanuts, sesame seeds, and previously fried garlic, and finely grind the Sichuan pepper, then set it aside.
  - Strain your oil with a spider strainer, then bring it up to around 155 °C.
  - Fill your jar* with the gochugaru and Cayenne pepper, then pour in the boiling oil, thoroughly straining out the infusion spices with a spider strainer. Then mix the oil and the chilies, and add your salt, sugar, peanuts, garlic, sesame seeds, Sichuan pepper, vinegar, and soy sauce.
  - Ideally, let rest for one to two days in the refrigerator so that the chili oil can develop its flavors.

## A recipe inspired by the Sichuan region

In Sichuan cuisine, chili oil is a central preparation used in countless dishes.
They do not use just one, but rather a wide range of different oils: Hóngyóu (红油), Málà hóngyóu (麻辣红油), Lǎoyóu (老油), Pàojiāo yóu (泡椒油), and so on.
Also, forget the image of chili oil whose sole purpose is to burn your palate: these oils bring an incredible amount of flavor and aroma.
This is not a step-by-step recipe for one of these oils, but rather a recipe inspired by these different preparations and their particular techniques, an in-between blend of Sichuan chili oil and chili crisp oil.

When choosing chilies, we are looking for three functions: heat, aroma and flavor, and color. So potentially three different chilies for their respective qualities.
Here, Gochugaru (Korean chili flakes) provides both color and aroma; it is only mildly spicy, but gives the oil a beautiful scarlet-red color, which is why we use so much of it.
Cayenne, on the other hand, brings significant heat, so use a slightly lighter hand and adjust the quantity according to your guests' tolerance.

The choice of oil is also important. There are two possibilities. Either use a neutral oil that is easy to find in supermarkets (grapeseed, rapeseed, or sunflower oil), or a more aromatic and traditional oil, which is also more difficult to find. Namely caiziyou (菜籽油), a toasted rapeseed oil.
This one is very common in Sichuan cuisine, with a powerful, toasted, slightly vegetal flavor and a mustard aroma when heated (unlike neutral oils, which are more odorless).

When serving the chili oil in your cooking or at the table, you can either scoop out only the oil from the top of the jar or take some of the solids, which are absolutely delicious and very fragrant.
