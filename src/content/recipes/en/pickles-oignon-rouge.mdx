## Origins

The English term _pickle_ appeared in the late Middle Ages. Its etymology is uncertain, but it is generally linked to the Middle Dutch _pekel_, meaning “brine”—salt water used to preserve food.

The word initially referred to a preparation associated with preserving food in salty liquid before taking on a broader meaning. Today, it describes any vegetable that has undergone a preservation method, whether fermentation, vinegar, or simply salt. The best-known and most widespread example is the gherkin, but any vegetable can be pickled, with a different flavor profile depending on the technique and the vegetable itself.

## The different classifications of pickles

Three broad families of pickles can be distinguished according to the dominant chemical process used to preserve the vegetable.

These families are not mutually exclusive. Salt is often used at the beginning of a lactic fermentation, and some pickles combine fermentation with the addition of vinegar. The third family also includes salted vegetables that do not ferment.

As with any attempt at classification, these categories should be taken with a grain of salt: the boundaries between them are often porous.

## A bright, fragrant condiment

After this brief preamble, let’s get our hands into the dish—or rather, the recipe! In terms of flavor profile, red onion pickles bring acidity, sweetness, and a certain bite to many dishes. They pair especially well with salads, cheese, and charcuterie. A nice touch: the vinegar takes on the onion’s red color, turning red or even pink, a color the pickles retain, enhancing a dish not only through flavor but also through appearance.

This brine balances sugar and vinegar, while the spices (fennel, cloves, bay leaf) add aromatic depth without masking the onion’s flavor. Use it to season and brighten any dish, especially to cut through richness.

The longer they rest, the more the flavors meld. They are ready after 24 hours. Once opened, keep refrigerated and use within a week.
