  - "Prep: cut your garlic and ginger and run them through a microplane (or chop them very finely), cut your scallions (separate the green from the white) and the cilantro if you like it, and chop your peanuts with a knife (or crush them in a mortar). Toast your Sichuan pepper in your wok for 2 to 3 minutes, then grind it in a mill or crush it in a mortar."
  - Take a bowl and mix the tahini (sesame paste), peanut butter, black vinegar, soy sauce and chili oil, one tablespoon of sugar, and half of the garlic, ginger and pepper.
  - Now get out your wok (or skillet) and brown your ground pork, you want a good Maillard reaction, so high heat and don't stir too often so it can color, add the other half of your garlic, ginger and pepper then deglaze with a little soy sauce and black vinegar. On one side add the white parts of the scallions. Let them color 5 more minutes.
  - Heat water for your noodles, follow the cooking time on the package, and just before draining them, scoop out 4 to 5 tablespoons of cooking water and stir it into the sauce to loosen it and dilute it a little.
  - Put the sauce in your bowl, then the noodles, then the meat, and finish with your toppings (crushed peanuts, the green part of the scallions and your cilantro) and a drizzle of toasted sesame oil. Eat while the noodles and meat are hot (the sauce is cold, so don't wait)!

## Origin

Dandan noodles are a Chinese dish from Sichuan province, a region known for its spicy cuisine that really heats up the palate. In Chinese the name is _Dandan Mian_ (carried noodles), where _dandan_ refers to the wooden or bamboo baskets that street noodle vendors carried on their backs using a shoulder pole. Baskets that held the famous noodles they sold right on the street.

## Rich & savory noodles

This noodle dish breaks down into 4 components (in the order they go in the bowl): the sauce, the noodles, the ground pork, and the toppings. The characteristic flavor is in the sauce, which is very bold, well spiced with Sichuan pepper (a very particular spice, it definitely heats things up but also has lemony notes, and what makes it special is the numbing side), balanced by the roundness from sesame and peanut paste (here you really need to watch the bitterness these can bring). On top of that you have soy sauce, black vinegar, and your chili oil. The dish can get bitter fast with the peanuts and very salty with the soy sauce, so watch that and don't salt the meat or your noodles.

For the meat, pork works really well (and it's traditional) because of how fatty it is. But ground pork isn't always easy to find at the supermarket (unlike beef). So go ask your butcher if they can run pork shoulder (or loin, for example) through the grinder, or go to Tang Frères (Asian supermarket in Paris, a real gold mine, use the trip to stock up on your Asian pantry)!

Get out your best wok (or skillet), because here you want a good Maillard reaction, really fry the pork in its own fat. Little tip: don't put too much meat in the wok at once or the temperature drops and the meat soaks up water, which stops browning and you get boiled meat more than anything. Otherwise just cook on high heat until the water cooks off, then leave it to color without touching for one to two minutes before stirring hard (repeat 4 to 5 times for even browning, it's a patience game).

This dish is simple to make, it's about balance between salt, bitterness and heat, so adjust for you and your guests, but I really stress how important good prep is: get your garlic, ginger and scallions ready ahead of time, then your sauce, and it'll all go like clockwork!
